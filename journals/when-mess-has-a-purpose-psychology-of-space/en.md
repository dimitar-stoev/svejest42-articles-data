We live in an era of intense visual scrutiny. Scroll through any social media feed, and you will be confronted with immaculate living rooms, hyper-organized pantries, and perfectly clear kitchen islands. The implicit message is consistent: a tidy home reflects a disciplined, successful mind, while a messy home is a sign of personal failure, stress, and chaos.

But what if this modern obsession with visual perfection is actually making our homes less functional?

A provocative [new study published in the peer-reviewed Journal of Business Research](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116581) in October 2026 challenges the assumption that visible disorder always represents a breakdown in organization. Instead, the researchers propose a more useful distinction: the difference between disruptive clutter that causes stress, and purposeful mess that actively supports daily routines, shared activities, and relationships.

## Rethinking the Meaning of Mess

To understand how people actually live, researchers Jamal Abarashi, Khaleel Edirisingha, and Amabel Errmann conducted in-depth photo-elicitation interviews with members of 22 multi-person households in New Zealand. Participants were asked to photograph five spaces they considered "tidy" and five they considered "messy," then explain how those spaces were used.

As [Abarashi explains](https://theconversation.com/the-upside-of-untidiness-why-a-little-mess-can-be-good-for-your-home-294343), the study treats order and disorder not as fixed reflections of a person's personality, but as changing states produced by the daily rhythms of life. The researchers identified three distinct categories of "productive" disorder:

### 1. Transitional Disorder
This is the temporary accumulation of objects that supports movement between activities. Consider the dining table used as an after-work "drop zone" for keys and mail, or a kitchen island where ingredients and tools remain visible so they won't be forgotten. This mess serves a distinct cognitive function: it acts as a physical reminder and absorbs the friction of transitioning from work to home.

### 2. Functional Disorder
This occurs in spaces forced to serve multiple purposes. The researchers noted instances where a single kitchen counter simultaneously supported dining, remote work, and a child's homework. The accumulated papers, toys, and laptops were tolerated as long as enough room remained to actually eat. The mess was the inevitable byproduct of a hard-working room.

### 3. Relational Disorder
Sometimes, leaving something out is a form of communication or care. In one compelling example from the study, a father left his daughter's jumper on a chair rather than putting it away. Why? Because moving it from the floor to the chair represented progress he wanted to encourage. Another participant tolerated greater disorder in a heavily used living room because it signaled a warm, lived-in, shared family space.

## The European Reality: When Space is a Luxury

The necessity of purposeful mess is particularly relevant in Europe, where spatial constraints often make showroom-level tidiness practically impossible. 

According to [Eurostat's 2025 edition on Housing in Europe](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/interactive-publications/housing-2025), 17% of the EU population lives in overcrowded homes. This pressure is distributed unevenly: the overcrowding rate reaches 33% in Bulgaria, 39% in Latvia, and 40% in Romania. The situation is even more acute for the next generation, with [26.9% of EU residents aged 15–29](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/en/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20260929-2) living in overcrowded conditions.

When a home has fewer rooms than household members, spaces must be exceptionally flexible. A single table may need to serve as a breakfast bar, a corporate desk, a homework station, and a dinner table all within twelve hours. In these environments, demanding permanent visual perfection is not just unrealistic—it actively obstructs the home's ability to function.

## The Fine Line Between Purpose and Chaos

While the new research offers a refreshing defense of lived-in spaces, it is crucial not to romanticize chronic chaos. The New Zealand study is qualitative and exploratory; it explains *how* people use mess, but it does not claim that living in a permanently messy home improves mental health.

In fact, a significant body of psychological research provides a necessary counterweight. A foundational [2010 study of 60 dual-income spouses](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167209352864) found that women who described their homes using stressful, "unfinished-home" language (focusing on clutter and disorganization) experienced flatter daily cortisol slopes and worsening depressed moods across the day.

Similarly, a [2016 survey of nearly 1,500 adults](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272494416300159) linked an overabundance of possessions to a weaker sense of psychological home and lower self-reported wellbeing. And a [2021 laboratory experiment involving 96 young women](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9216699/) demonstrated that a deliberately chaotic room—combined with crowding and loud noise—caused slightly higher physiological stress responses during a caregiving task compared to an orderly environment.

How do we reconcile these findings? The strongest synthesis is that the psychological impact of mess depends entirely on its *nature* and *scale*.

Purposeful disorder is active. It is attached to a current routine, involves items that are actively being used, and has a foreseeable endpoint. Stagnant clutter, on the other hand, consists of excessive possessions that no longer support a clear activity, creating cognitive friction, physical obstruction, and long-term stress.

## The Hidden Question: Who Restores the Order?

There is another vital caveat to the idea of "relational" and "functional" mess: a useful arrangement for one person may be experienced as an obstruction or unpaid labor by another. The New Zealand researchers noted that all households utilized strategies to restore order, but explicitly acknowledged they did not examine household power dynamics regarding *who* does that restoring.

This omission is significant. According to [Eurostat time-use statistics](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=How_do_women_and_men_use_their_time_-_statistics), women remain significantly more involved than men in cleaning, laundry, and household management across all measured European countries. 

Celebrating relaxed organizational standards is only healthy if the burden of managing those transitions is shared. If one partner's "transitional drop zone" becomes another partner's daily tidying chore, the mess is no longer purposeful—it is simply a transfer of work.

## Organizing for Real Life

Should a home be judged by how tidy it looks, or by how effectively its arrangement supports routines and relationships? The evidence suggests the latter. Rather than striving for an impossible minimalist ideal, households can adopt a more pragmatic approach to space management:

*   **Evaluate by function, not appearance:** Does an untidy area act as a reminder, support an active project, or enable a transition? Or does it prevent you from using the room and make objects hard to find?
*   **Differentiate active mess from stagnant accumulation:** If materials are out because you are working on a project this week, that is functional. If they have been sitting there for three months, that is clutter.
*   **Establish "reset" moments:** Instead of demanding permanent visual perfection, create natural resets. Clear the transitional surface before dinner, or pack away the mobile project boxes at the end of the working week.
*   **Negotiate shared spaces:** An arrangement is not genuinely functional if its convenience for one person creates repeated frustration for another. The threshold for acceptable mess must be agreed upon by everyone using the space.

Ultimately, a house is a machine for living, not a gallery for looking. By distinguishing between the clutter that drains us and the purposeful mess that serves us, we can stop apologizing for spaces that simply look like life is happening in them.
