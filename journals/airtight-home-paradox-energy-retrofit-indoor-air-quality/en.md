Energy renovation is almost universally presented as an uncomplicated win: lower bills, warmer rooms, and fewer carbon emissions. With [approximately 75% of EU buildings suffering from poor energy performance](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/topics/energy-efficiency/energy-performance-buildings/energy-performance-buildings-directive/energy-renovation-buildings_en) and an annual renovation rate stalling at just around 1%, the policy mandate is clear: Europe needs to insulate. 

But as millions of historic and aging buildings are wrapped in thermal layers and fitted with triple-glazed windows, a more complicated reality is emerging. A home functions as both a thermal envelope and a breathing system. By successfully sealing leaks to stop unwanted heat loss, we also drastically reduce uncontrolled air exchange. 

If no effective ventilation strategy replaces those old, drafty gaps, the pollutants generated by the ground, building materials, cooking, heating, and our everyday lives remain indoors much longer. The question facing architects, policymakers, and homeowners is not whether to halt retrofits, but how to prevent an energy upgrade from degrading the air we breathe.

## The Unintended Consequences of Sealing the Cracks

The physical tension is straightforward: insulation itself is not harmful, but an airtight building without adequate air renewal is. When researchers from the ARDEN project, led by Ann Marie Coggins at the University of Galway, [investigated deep energy renovations in Ireland](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/361449510/1-s2.0-S0360132324004797-main.pdf), they documented a profound, yet conflicting, transformation.

The thermal benefits were undeniable. Before the renovation, only 25% of occupants reported satisfaction with their winter thermal comfort. After the retrofits, that number soared to 100%. The homes were significantly warmer and vastly more energy-efficient. 

However, this newfound comfort came with a genuine trade-off. In the small sample of homes measured before and after the deep retrofits, levels of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and formaldehyde actually increased. After accounting for outdoor temperature, wind speed, and home identity, modeled PM2.5 concentrations rose by 11 to 26 μg/m³ in bedrooms and 18 to 26 μg/m³ in living rooms. 

The problem wasn't the insulation; it was the failure of the breathing system. Out of 11 installed ventilation systems assessed post-retrofit, [only two met the applicable minimum measured ventilation-flow performance requirement](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/361449510/1-s2.0-S0360132324004797-main.pdf). Researchers found systems operating at low pressure, vents clogged with dust, limited user access to controls, and residents who didn't fully understand how their systems worked.

As Nicky Hardy, a construction manager for Irish housing provider Tuath Housing, [recently warned regarding Ireland's lagging retrofit programme](https://www.rte.ie/news/ireland/2026/1108/1598434-retrofitting-ireland/), retrofitting historic buildings requires viewing the entire structure holistically. Insulating them incorrectly, or omitting adequate ventilation, can create long-term problems by making them far too airtight.

## The Invisible Threat: Radon and the Building Envelope

While formaldehyde and PM2.5 can sometimes be traced to indoor sources like furniture and cooking, radon presents a uniquely high-stakes geological challenge. 

Radon is an invisible, odorless radioactive gas that seeps from the ground. According to the [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/radon-and-health/), long-term lung cancer risk rises by approximately 16% for every 100 Bq/m³ increase in average radon concentration. Across the continent, the [European Code Against Cancer estimates](https://cancer-code-europe.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/10-european-code-against-cancer-radon-exposure-factsheet.pdf) that radon exposure is responsible for around 19,000 lung cancer deaths annually.

Because energy retrofits alter how air moves between the soil, the basement, and the living spaces, they can inadvertently change indoor radon levels. A comprehensive [Swiss investigation of 650 energy-efficient dwellings](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4433/10/12/777) revealed intriguing patterns. The geometric-mean radon concentration was 52 Bq/m³ in newly built Minergie (a Swiss energy standard) homes, but 87 Bq/m³ in renovated Minergie homes, and 105 Bq/m³ in other energy-renovated homes. Furthermore, the 118 fully retrofitted dwellings had a geometric mean of 131 Bq/m³, reported as 50% higher than partially retrofitted residences.

It is crucial to note that this association is not conclusive proof of causation. The Swiss study's differences were heavily influenced by building age, underlying geology, ground contact, and construction types. The strongest before-and-after subset in the study contained only 60 homes. While average radon in those specific homes was reported as 20% higher post-renovation, [that increase was not statistically significant at the conventional threshold (p = 0.15)](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4433/10/12/777). 

Nevertheless, some individual homes in the study saw their radon levels spike by four to eight times after renovation. The takeaway isn't that every retrofit raises radon, but that a retrofit inherently changes the building's dynamics. In high-radon-risk Swiss regions, the protective power of good design was clear: mechanically ventilated homes maintained a geometric mean of 96 Bq/m³, compared to a staggering 251 Bq/m³ in naturally ventilated homes.

## The Human Factor: How We Live Indoors

The way a home performs on paper often differs wildly from how it performs when occupied by people, pets, and modern habits. The Irish ARDEN study noted that pollutant increases could not be pinned solely on airtightness; researchers pointed to wood stoves, cooking, candles, air fresheners, pets, and outdoor pollution entering through open windows as major contributors.

This behavioral impact is reinforced by newer European monitoring data. An [October 2026 multi-country study published as a conference supplement](https://academic.oup.com/eurpub/article/36/Supplement_5/ckag169.339/8995227) by the K-HEALTHinAIR project observed substantial variation in indoor particles across Europe. They found especially high PM2.5 guideline-exceedance frequencies in Austrian wood-heated homes (56%), Spanish smokers' homes, and Polish electrically heated homes. Spanish homes using gas cooking logged an average PM2.5 concentration of 29.5 μg/m³.

Jose Fermoso, a chemical engineer and coordinator of K-HEALTHinAIR, emphasizes that [indoor pollution is notoriously difficult to address](https://www.engineersireland.ie/News/homing-in-on-the-invisible-pollution-in-homes-schools-and-workplaces) because most contaminants have no noticeable smell, and conditions vary room by room. As Fermoso notes, simply displaying numbers on a consumer air-quality monitor isn't enough; tools must translate measurements into understandable actions for residents.

## Policy and the Future of the European Home

Buildings should be evaluated not just by the kilowatts they save, but by the quality of life they provide. Dusan Licina, [head of EPFL's Human-Oriented Built Environment Lab](https://actu.epfl.ch/news/the-hidden-value-of-healthy-indoor-air/), recently argued that we must prioritize low-emission materials, meticulously maintained ventilation, radon protection, and continuous occupant feedback. Licina cautions against relying purely on opening windows, which is not a universal solution when outdoor pollution, wildfire smoke, or energy loss must be considered.

The legislative framework in Europe is beginning to catch up. The [revised EU Energy Performance of Buildings Directive](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/1275/2026-05-24/eng) explicitly defines indoor environmental quality to include temperature, humidity, ventilation rates, and contaminants. It mandates that Member States account for indoor environmental quality to avoid negative effects like inadequate ventilation during major renovations.

However, residential protection remains somewhat uneven. While air-quality measuring and control devices are now mandatory in specified zero-emission and deeply renovated non-residential buildings, Member States are only given the *option* to require equivalent devices in private homes. Will Europe measure the success of its renovation wave solely through energy certificates, or also through the air residents actually breathe?

## How to Renovate Without Ruining the Air

If you are planning to upgrade your home's thermal envelope—whether through new windows, wall insulation, or major air-sealing—the evidence points to several clear practical steps:

*   **Ventilation is not an accessory:** Treat ventilation design as a core part of the renovation, not a later addition. A fan merely being present does not guarantee that fresh air actually reaches bedrooms or that stale air effectively leaves the kitchen.
*   **Demand documentation:** Request documented ventilation design, commissioning results, measured airflow rates, and clear operating instructions from your installer.
*   **Test for Radon:** In radon-prone regions, check official maps and [arrange a long-duration measurement before renovating](https://cancer-code-europe.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/10-european-code-against-cancer-radon-exposure-factsheet.pdf). Test again after the building envelope changes. Radon cannot be detected by sight or smell, nor can it be measured by standard consumer CO2 sensors.
*   **Control the source:** Use effective extractor hoods while cooking, and minimize indoor pollutants like wood smoke, candles, incense, and aerosol air fresheners.
*   **Never block vents:** Do not permanently block wall vents or disable mechanical systems to stop noise or draughts. Those symptoms mean the system needs inspection, balancing, or repair, not deactivation.

Energy retrofits are essential for our climate future, our wallets, and our winter comfort. But a modern home shouldn't act like a plastic bag. A truly sustainable house is one that knows how to breathe.
